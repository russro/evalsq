# eval2 slide outline (first pass)

Talk: October 10, 2026. Figures come from `uv run evalsq --grid 25` (`figures/`). Diagram mockups come from `uv run python slides/mockups.py` (`slides/mock_*.png`) and are used as final figures in the deck.

1. **Title.** eval2: evaluating evals before they dictate deployment decisions. Russell Ro, October 10, 2026.
2. **What is an eval? What is a benchmark?** Eval: measuring how a system does on a task with one or more metrics, per example, per task, or whole system in deployment [1][2]. Benchmark: a fixed dataset + metric, shared to compare methods [3]. Every benchmark is an eval, most evals are not benchmarks. Either way it is a proxy for $, and it only counts while it keeps predicting $ [4][5].
3. **Pipeline.** Data → Models → Eval → Deploy → $. The $ arrives L days late, which is why we decide on the eval at all. Diagram: `mock_pipeline.png`.
4. **Drift.** Three ways the world moves under a fixed eval [10]. Anchor on the joint P(x,y), split two ways: P(x,y) = P(y|x)·P(x) = P(x|y)·P(y). Each drift is one factor moving while its partner holds. Data (covariate) drift: P(x) moves, P(y|x) holds (SPY: volatility jumps in 2020, 2022) [6][7][10]. Concept drift: P(y|x) moves, P(x) holds, the same inputs now mean something else (SPY: a signal that predicted up days stops working) [8][10]. Label drift: P(y) moves, P(x|y) holds, so by Bayes P(y|x) shifts too (SPY: share of up days differs in bull vs bear years, so accuracy moves even if the model doesn't) [9][10]. Notes only, toy label-drift numbers: P(signal|up)=0.7, P(signal|down)=0.4; bull P(up)=0.6 gives P(up|signal)≈0.72, bear P(up)=0.45 gives ≈0.59. The eval sits on top of all three, and the $ only shows up L days later. No figure, three small panels in draw.io. Refs: [6] Quiñonero-Candela et al. 2009, Dataset Shift in ML (MIT Press). [7] Moreno-Torres et al. 2012, A unifying view on dataset shift, Pattern Recognition 45(1). [8] Gama et al. 2014, A survey on concept drift adaptation, ACM CSUR 46(4). [9] Lipton, Wang, Smola 2018, Detecting and correcting for label shift with black box predictors, ICML. [10] Huyen 2022, Data Distribution Shifts and Monitoring, huyenchip.com/2022/02/07/data-distribution-shifts-and-monitoring.html.
5. **Worked example: SPY trading, but worse.** Setup: SPY daily, 2010–2025. Target is next-day up/down. Features: 1/5/20-day returns, MA10/MA50 gaps, 20-day vol, day/month. Model zoo (8): LogReg, LogReg-mom, RF, ExtraTrees-vol, GBM, GBM-mom, kNN, MLP (each gets its own feature subset). Monthly loop: (1) at month t, retrain each model and score it on data through t−L (L = 21 trading days); (2) the eval metric picks one model; (3) deploy it for the month; (4) the P&L only shows up L days later. Figures: `fig1_setup.png`, `mock_timeline.png`.
6. **Evaluating the eval.** (final text) Because of the L-day lag (for both observing data and making actions), we can't just deploy the model that made money in short time frames. We have to pick on a proxy metric (or combination of metrics). Treat each metric as a selection rule then run through the monthly loop. Grade each rule by the $ it earned from 2018 to 2025. Metrics: Accuracy, AUC, Negative log loss, Bear accuracy, Lagged P&L, Combined. Figure: `mock_bench_of_bench.png`.
7. **Three heuristics to consider.** H1. Validity: does the selector score track $? H2. Temporal holdout: does the best selector survive over time? H3. Complementarity: what do the evals say about each other, and does adding one add $ information? Diagram: `mock_heuristics_map.png` (update H3 box).
8. **H1a. Validity: does the selector score track $?** Accuracy correlates 0.82 with monthly Sharpe, AUC 0.35. AUC for LogReg falls to −0.55 in 2023. Figure: `fig2_h1_validity.png`.
9. **H1b. What each metric earns.** $100k deployed by each rule, net of 1bp per trade and 0.5%/yr short borrow: bear-day accuracy $320k, accuracy $209k, AUC $182k, log-loss $167k. Never switching made $344k and SPY made $287k. The metric with the best correlation does not make the most money. Fees cost the switching rules $10k to $15k (60+ swaps, 1 to 3 flips a month); at 10bp only bear-day accuracy ($220k) and never switching ($288k) are still close to SPY. Figures: `fig5_deploy.png`, `fig6_picks.png`.
10. **H2. Temporal holdout: does the best selector survive over time?** Same monthly P&L as H1b, cut into blocked folds of 6 months, 1 year and 2 years (never shuffled, so no fold sees a later regime). In each fold, rank the six rules by $. Figure: nested heatmap, each rule gets 3 rows (6mo/1y/2y), number = rank, color = annualized return. Shorter windows make the winner jumpier: last fold's winner repeats 13% of the time at 6mo (below chance, 17%) and 29% at 1y. Bear-day accuracy gets steadier as the window grows: avg rank 2.2 (6mo), 1.9 (1y), 1.5 (2y), and it is #1 or #2 in every 2y block. 2022-23 is red at every window; AUC wins 2022 and is the worst rule overall. Spoken line: "One split picks an eval. The next split usually picks a different one." Figure: `fig3_h2_folds.png`.
10b. **H2. What trusting the winner costs.** Regret = return points lost per fold vs the best rule in hindsight. Following last fold's winner loses 7.3 pts per half-year, 7.2 per year, 15.7 per 2y. At 6mo that is worse than a random rule (6.2); at 1y and 2y it beats random (10.9 / 22.4). Always using bear-day accuracy stays well ahead at every window (3.1 / 3.1 / 4.7). Combined is the worst fixed rule (7.5 / 14.4 / 32.3). Point: a robust eval is the one that is never far from best, not the one that wins most often. Caveats: 4 to 16 folds is a stability check, not a significance test, and picking bear-day accuracy as "the fixed rule" is itself hindsight. Figure: `fig3b_h2_regret.png`. Old 2-model H2 (winner flips 3 times, RF wins 2022) moved to notes; its yearly table still feeds the old-H3 note (12d).
11. **H3. Complementarity: what do the evals say about each other?** Intro (spoken): "So far we graded each eval on whether it signals money (H1) and whether that holds up over time (H2). Now we look at them together: what do they say about each other, and how much information do they add in general?" An eval suite works like a portfolio: two correlated evals are one bet counted twice. No figure, or a 3-box recap (alone / over time / together).
11a. **H3a. Redundancy: which evals are secretly the same eval?** Within-month rank corr over the 8 models, averaged over 96 months (2018-25). Two clusters: "did it get it right" (accuracy + lagged P&L, 0.67) and "is it calibrated" (AUC + log-loss, 0.63). Across clusters they barely agree (accuracy vs AUC 0.24). Bear-day accuracy sits near the first cluster (0.49 / 0.44) but is far from the calibration pair (0.16 / 0.21). Pick agreement tells the same story: accuracy and lagged P&L deploy the same model 56% of months, AUC and log-loss 47%, bear-day accuracy and AUC 11%. Bottom row: none of the evals ranks models by that month's $ (all within ±0.05) except bear-day accuracy (0.09). Point: the evals talk to each other far more than they talk to $. Figure: `fig11_h3_redundancy.png`.
11b. **H3b. Complementarity: does adding an eval add $?** Combine a subset of evals the plain way (mean rank, same as Combined) and deploy. Greedy forward selection: start with the best single eval and keep adding whichever helps most. Bear-day accuracy alone $320k; + log-loss $226k; + accuracy $232k; + AUC $219k; all five $167k (net). The curve falls: every add costs money because averaging dilutes the one eval that carries $ information (adding anything to bear-day accuracy costs $95k to $146k). Among the weaker evals, cross-cluster pairs help (AUC + lagged P&L +$64k, log-loss + accuracy +$46k) while the same-cluster pair does not (accuracy + lagged P&L −$29k). Point: more evals is not more information. Spread helps only if you weight for it, which sets up the learned eval. Figure: `fig11b_h3_complement.png`.
12. **H3c. Learn the weights instead? Learned eval and why it fails.** Ridge on the six eval signals, demeaned across the zoo, predicting next-month return minus the zoo average. No model identity, so it learns which signals matter. Trained on 2012 to 2017 months too. Refit monthly on all history $177k, on the last 36 months $191k, frozen after 36 months $149k, all net. None beat accuracy ($209k), bear-day accuracy ($320k) or never switching ($344k). Rank IC about 0 (leave one model out 0.04, t 0.9), the same story as the 0.02 within-month corr. The all-history weights settle on accuracy minus uptrend accuracy, which is close to bear-day accuracy. The frozen fit leans on lagged P&L, which flips sign after 2023: drift kills the static selector. Figure: `fig10_learned_weights.png`.

12b. **Where learned evals do work: EmbedLLM / Bullpen.** This is H3 at the question level: drop redundant questions, keep the informative ones, probe a new model with k items to predict its full score. Task: hundreds of LLMs by thousands of benchmark questions, scored 0/1 correct. Learn an embedding per model and per question, then predict whether model M gets question Q right. Useful for (1) routing, sending each query to the cheapest model likely to get it right, and (2) estimating a new model's scores from a small subset instead of running the full suite. It works there because questions come from a fixed pool (exchangeable), a released model does not change, and the matrix is big. Stocks break all three: tomorrow is not like yesterday, models retrain, and we have 96 rows. Takeaway: a learned eval only transfers while new items look like the old ones. No figure.
12c. **The more you compare, the more the leaderboard lies (winner's curse).** Best of k GBM configs: the eval promises 6.2pt more than it delivers at k=25, 0.1pt at k=1. Eval climbs 54% → 61% as k grows, deployed stays near 54% for every k. Why: within a month, eval acc vs deployed acc correlates 0.02, so the k=25 winner is just the luckiest config on the 63-day window and falls back to the average. The configs are nearly tied anyway (52 to 56% deployed), and the month matters more than the model (spread 9.3pt across months vs 5.4pt between models in a month). Month to month the gap swings −25 to +30pt and the best k flips between 1 and 25 with no pattern. Figures: `fig7_winners_curse.png`, `fig8_gap_dist.png`, `fig9_opt_k.png`.
12d. **Notes only: old H3, can the market alone predict the score?** Predict RF yearly accuracy from that year's volatility and trend. LOO R² = −0.38 yearly, −0.03 monthly, so the score is not just a market thermometer. Figure: `fig4_h3_meta.png` (backup).
13. **Insights. What predicts a good eval, and what doesn't.** Predictive: plain accuracy (corr 0.82 with Sharpe, and it stays above 0.62 in every rolling window); scoring on the regime you fear (bear-day accuracy earns the most of any rule, $320k net); grading the eval across many time folds instead of one split (H2: last fold's best eval repeats only 29% of the time). Not predictive: number of candidates k (more k inflates the bench, deployed stays flat); AUC (0.34, goes negative in 2023); combining every metric into one score (0.75 corr, $167k net, worse than accuracy alone; H3: every eval added to bear-day accuracy costs $95k+); a learned selector under drift ($149k to $191k net, rank IC about 0); a model's bench score within a month (0.02 corr with deployed). Caveat: correlation is not money. No rule beat never switching ($344k net), and the most correlated metric was not the most profitable.
14. **What each check catches.** H1 catches concept drift (the score to $ link moves). H2 catches drift in which eval works (regime change reorders the selectors, not just the models). H3 catches double counting and blind spots: evals that are secretly the same, and averaging that dilutes the one informative eval. A learned eval can fix the weighting only while items stay exchangeable. The winner's curse is selection bias, which shows up even with no drift.
15. **Discussion.** Many more checks depend on the task: coverage audits, label consistency, contamination. Questions for the room: "Which 2 of your evals are secretly the same eval?" and "What drift are you most exposed to, and would your eval notice?" Audience takeaways: report a correlation matrix for your eval suite like you would for a portfolio; before adding eval #N, check it adds information instead of duplicating one you have.
